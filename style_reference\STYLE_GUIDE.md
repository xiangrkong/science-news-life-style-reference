# Science News Life Style Guide

Status: baseline version based on the Life topic page and two current articles reviewed on August 26, 2026. Confidence is provisional until more articles are sampled.

## Editorial profile

Write for an interested general reader. Lead with a concrete question, familiar situation, surprising observation, or concise finding, then move quickly to the evidence. Make the science vivid without overstating what the study establishes.

## Voice and tone

- Clear, conversational, evidence-led, and accessible to an informed general audience.
- Use curiosity and restrained surprise to create momentum.
- Prefer precise wording over hype; match the strength of verbs to the evidence.
- Explain specialist terms at first use with a short apposition, analogy, or concrete example.
- Let occasional plainspoken phrases relieve technical density, but keep the overall tone professional.

## Sentence and paragraph patterns

- Open with a short narrative hook or direct question, then state the new finding within the first two paragraphs.
- Alternate compact declarative sentences with longer explanatory ones.
- Keep paragraphs short, usually advancing one idea, result, limitation, or quotation at a time.
- Move from the headline finding to background, methods, quantified results, caveats, and implications.
- Use quotations to interpret results or mark uncertainty, not merely to repeat nearby facts.
- Translate scale with familiar comparisons when useful.

## Vocabulary and mechanics

- Prefer active verbs such as `shows`, `suggests`, `reveals`, `found`, `tracked`, and `measured`.
- Calibrate uncertainty with `may`, `might`, `appears`, `is associated with`, and `it is unclear`.
- Favor concrete nouns and verbs over abstract nominalizations.
- Use em dashes or brief appositives to define terms without stopping the narrative.
- Headlines commonly pair an accessible claim or question with cautious modality.

## Do / avoid

Do:

- Put the main finding early and attach it to the relevant organism, population, or experiment.
- Include sample sizes, percentages, time spans, and comparison groups when material.
- Separate association from causation and surface study limitations.
- Use analogies only when they clarify mechanism or scale.

Avoid:

- Sensational certainty, promotional adjectives, and unsupported universals.
- Dense blocks of technical terminology.
- Hiding caveats until the end.
- Copying distinctive sentences or closely imitating any one author.

## Editing modes

### Light edit

Keep the original organization and personality. Make only necessary changes for grammar, clarity, concision, transitions, and factual precision.

### High-fidelity rewrite

Rebuild organization, rhythm, transitions, and diction to match the guide's abstract characteristics. Preserve the original meaning, facts, and level of certainty. Do not imitate or reproduce any single article verbatim.
